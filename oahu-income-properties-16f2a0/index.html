Paying cash

You pay the full price with no mortgage. Your return is the yearly net operating income (NOI) divided by the price, called the cap rate. It is the standard way investors compare buildings.

Using a loan

You put money down and the rents also pay the mortgage. We show cash on cash return (yearly cash flow after the mortgage divided by the cash you put in), monthly cash flow, the cap rate, and DSCR, the income to mortgage ratio lenders check (most want about 1.20 to 1.25 or higher). A loan helps when the property earns more than the loan costs and hurts when it does not.

How the numbers work

Every figure here is an estimate for comparing properties, not a promise of income, appreciation or loan approval.

Income

  • Listing-reported: the income, NOI or cap rate stated in the MLS listing by the seller's agent, shown with its basis. When a listing gives more than one figure, the lower one is used. Sellers' figures often leave out some costs such as vacancy, management or reserves, so where the MLS income field is the only source we also show what the cap rate would be if that figure were gross rent. Always ask for the rent roll and 24 months of operating statements.
  • Estimate: HUD fiscal year 2026 Fair Market Rents for Urban Honolulu by bedroom (studio $1,877, one bedroom $2,016, two bedroom $2,642, three bedroom $3,674 a month), reduced 10% for average condition, 20% for fair condition and another 10% for buildings from before 1960. Units with no bedroom count are modeled as two bedrooms.

Costs taken out of estimates

  • 5% vacancy, Hawaii GET 4.712% on rents, property tax by zoning (apartment $3.50, commercial and industrial $12.40, residential $4.00 up to $1M and $11.40 above, per $1,000 of assessed value), insurance $1,500 per unit, repairs 8%, reserves 5%, common utilities $120 per unit a month, accounting $1,000, and an 8% property manager.
  • Not included: renovation, special assessments, closing costs in the cash scenario, and any tax benefits.

Cash vs loan math

  • All cash: cap rate = NOI ÷ price.
  • Loan: monthly principal and interest on the price minus your down payment. Cash you put in = down payment + about 2% of the price for closing costs. Cash on cash = (NOI − yearly mortgage payments) ÷ cash you put in. DSCR = NOI ÷ yearly mortgage payments. Properties that would lose money in your scenario are hidden.
  • Duplex to fourplex default 7.75%, 25% down, 30 years: Freddie Mac's 30 year average of 7.18% (Nov 5, 2026) plus a typical premium for investment property loans.
  • 5+ units, mixed use and commercial default 7.75%, 30% down, 25 year amortization: the 10 year Treasury yield of 4.98% (U.S. Treasury, Nov 10, 2026) plus a typical commercial lending spread of about 2.5 to 3 points. Commercial loans often have 5 to 10 year terms with a balloon payment and need a DSCR of about 1.20 to 1.25. Your lender's quote will differ.
  • Est. total return. Cash: cap rate + the area's 10 year yearly appreciation. Loan: (first year cash flow + first year principal paid down + one year of appreciation on the full price at the 10 year pace) ÷ cash you put in. Leverage magnifies appreciation in both directions.

Tips for new investors

  • Start with the cap rate to compare buildings, then test the loan view. At today's rates many buildings only cash flow with a larger down payment.
  • Price per unit and rents per unit tell you more than the total price. Compare a few buildings in the same area.
  • Duplex to fourplex: you can often live in one unit and rent the rest, which can open owner occupant loan options. Ask a lender.
  • Check the leases: month to month tenants, below market rents and who pays utilities all change the real return.
  • Before an offer: rent roll, 24 months of statements, unit legality and permits, zoning, and a building inspection.
  • Talk with a CPA about taxes, depreciation and 1031 exchanges, and with an attorney about legal questions. Nothing here is tax, legal or lending advice.
